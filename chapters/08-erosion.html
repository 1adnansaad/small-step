Step 8 · River erosion

Rivers that move

Bangladesh's rivers do not stay where the map puts them. Every year they take land — and the households standing on it — with them.

The morphology of the country's rivers is highly dynamic, and bank erosion is a regular phenomenon, particularly along the banks of the main rivers.

A major reason for the erosion is that discharge in the rivers is increasing.

The peaks are changing shape

Fifty years of flow records tell three different stories, and each one matters for a different reason.

Bahadurabad — Brahmaputra / Jamuna

Peak discharge is increasing and peaking earlier. The average timing of the peak has moved from the middle of August to the first week of August.

Bhairab Bazar — Meghna

Peak discharge is decreasing and its occurrence is slightly delayed. The time of the peak has moved from mid-July in the late 1970s to the last week of September.

Hardinge Bridge — Ganges

Peak discharge is increasing and the time of the peak is advancing by about one day per decade.

What rising peaks mean Rising peak discharges on the Ganges and the Brahmaputra mean an increasing probability of future river erosion.
One consolation If the present trend of advancing peaks continues, the chance that the Ganges and Brahmaputra peaks coincide falls — reducing the probability of catastrophic, long-duration floods.

Land lost, land gained

River movement and sediment transport also build new land. Between 1973 and 2015 a total of 52,313 hectares were accreted.

The balance is still negative On balance, land accretion is significantly lower than land erosion for all three major rivers — and net erosion is largest for the Brahmaputra / Jamuna. Climate change is expected to make these rivers more dynamic still.
