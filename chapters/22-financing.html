Step 22 · Financing options

Where the money comes from

Public financing has to rise from 0.8% to about 2% of GDP. The plan is candid that finding the extra 1.2% of GDP will not be easy, even with optimistic assumptions about domestic resource mobilisation.

Public sector financing

The strategy is a combination of three things:

1 · Tax financing

Within the overall public investment programme, alongside the fiscal deficit and the domestic and external borrowing strategies set out in the plan — all kept consistent with internal and external stability.

2 · Cost recovery on the beneficiary pays principle

International best practice in delta management shows how strongly this principle features in financing water investments.

3 · Foreign funding, including the Green Climate Fund

The GCF was established to mobilise climate finance for scaled-up mitigation and adaptation in developing countries.

The Dutch example

In the Dutch delta, much of the funding for flood control, irrigation, water supply, sanitation and waste management comes from the beneficiary pays principle. O&M is fully funded through cost recovery, and water management is heavily decentralised.

80% of Dutch water spending is done by lower levels of government
100% of O&M is done at those lower levels, run by local water management bodies as private cooperative enterprises

Those bodies apply the beneficiary pays principle in full, with some cross-subsidy to recognised poorer communities.

Where Bangladesh stands today

  • O&M funding of urban water and sanitation is prevalent — but full cost recovery of O&M is not.
  • Cost recovery of O&M for waste management is negligible.
  • There is, by default, no recovery of capital cost for urban water supply, sanitation or waste management.
  • For flood control and irrigation there is no cost recovery of either capital cost or O&M — partly because local water management bodies do not exist.
What has to change Once local water management bodies are created under the proposed Delta Act, the beneficiary pays principle becomes possible for flood control and irrigation. For urban water and sanitation the institutions and regulations already exist — they need to be applied more rigorously, with a time-bound policy requiring all public urban water and sanitation services to gradually recover 100% of O&M cost. Over time, capital cost recovery can be considered, starting with the better-off service areas of the four WASAs — Dhaka, Chattogram, Khulna and Rajshahi. For solid waste, cost recovery can come through an annual service charge linked to a modern property tax system.
US$ 2bn / year the scale of funding Bangladesh may receive from the Green Climate Fund if it pursues its case effectively

Bangladesh has had significant success using public sector intermediaries to incentivise private business, particularly in renewable energy — which places it well to tap this grant funding and scale up delta investment.

Private sector financing

BDP 2100 projects that Bangladesh should be able to mobilise at least 0.5% of GDP per year from the private sector for water resource management and related infrastructure.

Five prospective areas

  • Water treatment, water supply and sewage treatment — international experience shows excellent prospects.
  • Irrigation.
  • Dredging — there is strong private interest, and costs may be significantly offset by proceeds from sand sales, though technical factors and risk sharing need careful management.
  • Land reclamation — combining it with river dredging in a PPP concession framework would be attractive to investors.
  • River port infrastructure for inland water transport.

On dredging in particular, the plan advises learning from international best practice in developing the right contracting arrangements.
