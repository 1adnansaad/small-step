Step 11 · Adverse impacts

What all of this costs

The climate factors work through a large number of sectors that add up to substantial economy-wide losses. Agriculture takes the first and heaviest hit.

Agriculture

High temperature reduces yields of high-yielding varieties of Aus, Aman and Boro rice. Changes in temperature, humidity and radiation also increase the incidence of insect pests, diseases and microorganisms.

Salinity on the farm

Soil salinity compounds the damage, and its effects are already severe. In Patuakhali, average rice yield is 40% below the national average and more than 50% below Naogaon. Under a moderate climate change scenario, crop loss from salinity intrusion could be about 0.2 million tonnes per year.

Forests and ecosystems

Sea level rise, higher temperatures and more intense cyclones will damage forest resources, pressure climate-sensitive species and worsen erosion and soil quality in upland forested areas.

The Sundarbans The world's largest mangrove forest is extremely vulnerable to climate change. Sea level rise will increase saltwater intrusion and negatively affect the forest and its diverse ecosystem.

Land, assets, infrastructure and health

  • At one metre of sea level rise, a significant part of Bangladesh's dryland is permanently inundated; the resulting land-quantity shock cuts production across every sector and lowers real GDP.
  • Under BAU, real GDP falls by 0.73% per year in 2030, rising to 0.93% per year by 2100, from that effect alone.
  • Capital stock in the construction sector would be depleted by an estimated 0.05% annually, with additional losses to road infrastructure.
  • Water-borne diseases (diarrhoea, dysentery) and vector-borne diseases (malaria, dengue) are climate sensitive — projections show growing morbidity from dengue and malaria.

The macro number

−1.1% of GDP per year in a moderate climate change environment
−2.0% of GDP per year in an extreme climate environment

BDP 2100's own projection, using the moderate environment, shows a loss of about 1.1% of GDP per year between 2017 and 2041, measured against the government's 9% annual average growth target. With the Delta Plan and its investment programme in place, average growth accelerates to 8.8% — an average annual GDP gap of 1.9% between doing nothing and doing this.

Hazard and poverty travel together

District and sub-district analysis shows a strong positive correlation between the incidence of poverty and the intensity of natural hazards.

  • 70% of the 16 districts ranked most exposed to natural disasters (risk category 1) have poverty rates above the national average.
  • Among high-risk districts (category 2), 67% are above the national poverty rate and 13% are level with it.
  • Of the 15 most poverty-stricken districts nationally, almost 90% fall in hazard risk category 1 or 2.
  • On per capita income, 80% of the most hazard-prone districts (category 1) sit below the national average — 67% for category 2, and 100% for category 3.
This is the case for the plan Protecting the delta is not separate from reducing poverty. In Bangladesh they are the same map.
