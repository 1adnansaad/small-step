Step 2 · The gap

Why the old way of planning fell short

Bangladesh was never short of plans. It was short of one plan that joined them together.

Over the years there have been many sectoral initiatives for water and agriculture, alongside national strategies such as the Five Year Plans and the Perspective Plan. Each was competent within its own boundary. The difficulty was integrating the sectoral, national and global pieces into a single view of the delta.

Three structural problems

They were too short term

Sectoral plans tend to be short term oriented — planned and implemented independently of one another, on horizons far shorter than the processes they are trying to control.

They worked in isolation

Water, land use, ecosystems, agriculture, transport and climate change were treated as separate subjects, even though in a delta a decision in one changes the outcome in all the others.

They assumed a predictable future

Climate change and socio-economic change carry large uncertainties. A plan that commits to one fixed forecast risks building the wrong thing at great expense.

What was needed instead A long term vision, with planning and implementation that involve every relevant sector at once — and that stay flexible as the future reveals itself.

Learning from other deltas

Other delta countries had faced the same problem first. Drawing on those international experiences — the Netherlands most directly, through technical assistance from the Dutch government — Bangladesh developed the BDP 2100: an integrated, holistic and adaptive techno-economic plan covering water resources, climate change and the environment together.
