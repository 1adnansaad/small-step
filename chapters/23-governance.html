Step 23 · Governance and institutions

Who runs the delta

A cross-sectoral plan needs a cross-sectoral institution. This is the part of BDP 2100 that decides whether the rest of it happens.

Implementation involves multiple line ministries, local government institutions, communities and the private sector. Clarity of role, interdependence of action and a coordinated approach are essential. Resources are limited and demands compete — how they are allocated, how trade-offs are made, and how effectively programmes are delivered are political economy questions that depend on the governance environment.

The starting position

Bangladesh has a long history of water management, but its main focus has been the protection of agriculture — understandable given agriculture's role in food security and the livelihoods of the poor. One consequence was less attention to climate change, river transport, urban water supply, and drainage and pollution of rivers and other water bodies.

The legal framework

The Water Act 2013 is comprehensive and treats water as an integrated resource — its objective is to “make provisions for integrated development, management, abstraction, distribution, use, protection and conservation of water resources”. Being recent, its associated regulatory policies and institutions have not fully taken shape, but it provides a strong basis. It currently operates through principles set by the National Water Policy 1999 and the National Water Resource Management Plan 2001 (adopted 2004).

Three long-standing gaps Implementation has suffered from political, financing and institutional constraints; local water management bodies are absent, so beneficiaries are missing from decision making; and water is poorly integrated with climate change, environment and land management because institutional coordination is inadequate.

Three objectives for institutional reform

  1. Establish effective institutions of inter-sectoral planning, programming, coordination and monitoring.
  2. Incorporate climate change, environment, biodiversity, agriculture, fisheries, forestry, inland water transport and land management — and their interaction with water — into a comprehensive view of the delta.
  3. Convert each related institution into a strong institution with adequate technical skills in economic management, financing, institution building, monitoring and evaluation, and knowledge management.

The machinery

Delta Governance Council (DGC)

A small but high-level inter-ministerial forum chaired by the Prime Minister, with the Planning Minister as Vice-Chair. It supervises and guides, provides the formal linkage for political commitment to BDP 2100, makes decisions, and issues strategic advice and policy guidelines.

Delta Plan Project / Programme Selection Committee (PPSC)

Chaired by Member, GED, with representatives of Finance, Planning, Water, Environment and Forestry, Agriculture, Land, Livestock and Fisheries, and Shipping. It facilitates the selection of projects and programmes for the Delta Plan Investment Programme.

General Economics Division (GED)

Assigned overall coordination, facilitation and M&E of BDP 2100 implementation, with a well-structured wing to be created and its institutional capacity enhanced. GED manages planning and research, coordinates implementation including investment plan selection, monitors progress and updates the plan. Actual implementation of approved programmes is carried out by the related ministries and implementing agencies.

Bangladesh Delta Fund

Initiated by consolidating current ADP spending of 0.8% of GDP, rising gradually to 2.5% by 2030. GED prepares and updates the delta spending programme annually, covering investments, O&M, research, capacity building and institutional development. Approved programmes are guaranteed funding for the full life of the project, with a proper balance between new projects and completing ongoing ones. Over time, O&M funding and smaller projects transfer to municipalities and local water management bodies, leaving the Fund managing a small number of national high-priority projects, innovation and research.

Local water management bodies

The missing institution in Bangladesh water management: representation of beneficiary stakeholders in decisions about coastal management, river management, freshwater wetlands (haors and baors), large irrigation schemes and flood control. These bodies must fundamentally represent stakeholders, not government interests, and should be established on a careful review of international experience, analysis of why past water users' associations failed, and further stakeholder consultation.

Strengthening what already exists

Core delta institutions needing immediate attention include GED, BWDB, WARPO, DOE, DOF, DAE, LGED, FD and DBHW, the municipalities (City Corporations and Pourashavas), the WASAs, the new local water management bodies, and the specialised delta-related institutions inside non-water ministries. BWDB and WARPO in particular need strengthening in technology, innovation, integrated planning, research, economic management and consultative process — and BWDB must learn to work with the new local bodies as a complementary institution rather than a competing one.

Cross-boundary dialogue As a lower riparian, Bangladesh faces real risks from upstream developments. The capacity of the Joint Rivers Commission must be considerably strengthened and its partnership with GED, WARPO and BWDB deepened. Routine dialogue — pleasantries, general complaints, information sharing — must give way to strategic dialogue aimed at problem solving, backed by multi-purpose technical options and cost-benefit analysis. The plan gives this the same priority as large water infrastructure projects.

Knowledge and monitoring

Creating a Delta Knowledge Bank is a prerequisite for adaptive delta management. GED will create a knowledge unit to collate delta knowledge into a digitised library, establish a delta data bank, and run a comprehensive knowledge and data updating effort — starting with a quick stock take, then a 3–5 year data and research work programme with annual targets, approved by the DGC. Data work runs with the Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics; research can be outsourced to universities and local research institutions.

Five elements of the knowledge approach

  1. Knowledge needs & agenda — identifying nine key subjects as burning issues, with the most important research questions.
  2. Knowledge accumulation — beginning with the 26 Baseline Studies, to be updated as gaps are found.
  3. Knowledge availability — through a web-based information portal holding geographic data layers, studies, policy documents and technical reports.
  4. Value realization — putting delta knowledge to use, for example as input to the 8th Five Year Plan and the Climate Atlas.
  5. Delta Knowledge Community — academics, policy makers, international donors, NGOs and field workers engaging continuously.

On monitoring and evaluation: M&E of investment projects rests with implementing agencies, M&E of sectoral policies with the concerned ministry and IMED, and M&E of overall BDP 2100 implementation with GED — done consultatively, drawing on project and sectoral analysis and involving all inter-ministerial agencies and IMED.

And then it starts again Because BDP 2100 is adaptive and long term, it must be integrated with the country's planning process — periodically and regularly reviewed, incorporated into future Five Year Plans as routine, updated with new knowledge and technologies, and continuously monitored and evaluated.
